# 06 — Company, Economy, and Logistics

## Growth path

The economy begins as the practical cost and reward structure around spacecraft. It later becomes a simulated network whose shortages, routes, facilities, and competitors generate missions.

The user’s economic priority is:

1. mining;
2. transport logistics;
3. manufacturing chains;
4. workforce;
5. contracts;
6. private companies;
7. maintenance;
8. supply-demand pricing.

Contracts appear early as progression scaffolding even though deeper contract simulation ranks below industrial systems in the long-term design.

## Company layer

The company owns money, designs, facilities, vehicles, inventory, contracts, knowledge, relationships, and eventually subsidiaries or remote operations. Early management should remain light enough that construction and flight dominate.

The initial company operates a fictional commercial launch facility near 28 degrees north on Florida's Atlantic coast, geographically distinct from real launch complexes. It owns:

- a small spacecraft assembly/integration hangar;
- a mission-control room;
- one launch pad;
- limited component and integrated-vehicle test equipment.

It leases or contracts major part manufacturing, specialized testing, long-range tracking/communications, transport, and other services beyond this small footprint. This creates early operating costs and partner dependencies without requiring the player to build an industrial base before the first launch. Exact starting inventory, staff, cash, service contracts, longitude/terrain placement, facility name, and regulatory arrangement remain open.

Possible later pressures include wages, financing, insurance, reputation, licenses, launch-site access, procurement lead times, and cash flow. They are not all approved features.

## Industrial chain

The proposed mature flow is:

```text
survey → claim/access → extract → refine → manufacture → assemble
       → store → transport → consume/maintain/recycle
```

Location and transport time matter. A kilogram in orbit is not economically equivalent to a kilogram on Earth or at an asteroid. Depots, tugs, shipyards, and local manufacturing create value by changing that geography.

## Markets and competitors

Other private companies should eventually operate assets, fulfill demand, bid for contracts, buy/sell goods, and expand. A completely simulated economy is not assumed; agent-based and aggregate market models should be compared against performance and gameplay needs.

Supply-demand pricing is deliberately later. Prices should respond to real availability, delivery time, risk, contracts, and production capacity without becoming an opaque high-frequency trading simulation.

## Strategic command

Strategic tools become necessary when the company has more simultaneous work than the player can personally fly: schedules, route templates, mission profiles, standing orders, staffing, maintenance queues, inventory policy, and alerts. Automation should be observable and interruptible.

## Decisions

| Decision | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mining/logistics/manufacturing are the economic spine | Confirmed | User priority order |
| Contracts enter early in reduced form | Proposed | Gives the first company clear opportunities and rewards |
| Strategic command unlocks when multiple operations demand it | Confirmed direction | User wants flight first, strategy later |
| Initial small Florida Atlantic-coast facility and owned assets above | Confirmed initial scope | User accepted recommendation |
| Facility region near 28 degrees north and distinct from real launch complexes | Confirmed direction | User accepted recommendation; exact terrain placement remains open |
| Lease major manufacturing and tracking services initially | Confirmed initial scope | Creates useful company constraints without front-loading industry |
| Exact market simulation and company finance depth | Open | High complexity and pacing impact |
